\label{ch:unbalance}
\section{Introduction}
The previous chapters presented the modeling and control of the three-phase active front-end (AFE) rectifier under balanced grid conditions. Chapter~2 introduced the rectifier model and vector-oriented control, while Chapter~3 discussed finite-control-set and modulated model predictive control approaches. Under balanced sinusoidal operation, the three grid voltages have equal magnitudes and are separated by $120^\circ$. In this case, the fundamental voltage and current components can be represented as constant quantities in a synchronous reference frame, which simplifies the design of the current controller and the regulation of active and reactive power.

In practical power systems, however, the grid voltages may become unbalanced because of asymmetrical faults, unequal loads, or differences in the impedances of the three phases. Under these conditions, the grid voltage contains both positive- and negative-sequence components. The negative-sequence component rotates in the opposite direction to the positive-sequence component and appears as a double-frequency oscillation in the positive synchronous reference frame. Consequently, a controller designed for balanced operation may experience oscillations in its measured variables and increased difficulty in tracking the desired grid currents.

Voltage unbalance also affects the power exchanged between the grid and the rectifier. The interaction between positive- and negative-sequence voltage and current components can produce oscillations in the instantaneous active and reactive powers. Active-power oscillations may, in turn, cause a double-frequency ripple in the DC-link voltage. Depending on the selected current references, unbalanced operation can also lead to unequal phase currents and increased current stress on the converter components. These effects make it necessary to consider current quality, power oscillations, and DC-link voltage regulation together when evaluating the performance of the AFE rectifier.

The control objectives under unbalanced conditions are not always independent. For a conventional three-phase, three-wire converter, the available current components limit the number of objectives that can be achieved simultaneously. For example, maintaining balanced grid currents and eliminating active-power oscillations may require different current references. The resulting choice also depends on the converter's current and voltage limits. Ma et al.~\cite{ma2015} discuss these controllability limitations. Their findings provide a useful basis for examining the trade-offs of the three-wire AFE considered in this thesis.

This chapter extends the analysis of the previous chapters to unbalanced grid operation. It first describes the positive- and negative-sequence components and their representation in stationary and synchronous reference frames. It then examines their effects on grid currents, instantaneous power, and DC-link voltage that arise under voltage unbalance. This analysis establishes the background for assessing and developing AFE rectifier control strategies under non-ideal grid conditions.

\section{Transformations and sequence components}
For a phase quantity \(x_{abc}=[x_a,x_b,x_c]^T\), the amplitude-invariant Clarke transformation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch4-clarke}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\alpha\\x_\beta\\x_0\end{bmatrix}=
\frac23\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac12&-\frac12\\0&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\\\frac12&\frac12&\frac12\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_a\\x_b\\x_c\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Its inverse is
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}x_a\\x_b\\x_c\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\-\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\\-\frac12&-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&1\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\alpha\\x_\beta\\x_0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The zero-sequence variable is the arithmetic mean of the phase quantities. Zero-sequence voltage may exist relative to a chosen reference; however, a conventional three-wire AFE has \(i_a+i_b+i_c=0\), so zero-sequence line current cannot flow. Other topologies with an appropriate return path permit additional control variables \cite{ma2015}.

Let \(\underline x=x_\alpha+jx_\beta\), \(\theta=\omega t+\theta_0\), and \(\underline X^\pm=x_d^\pm+jx_q^\pm\). Under a fundamental-frequency steady-state model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:seq}
\underline x=\underline X^+e^{j\theta}+\underline X^-e^{-j\theta}.
\end{equation}
Positive and negative sequences rotate in opposite directions because of their opposite phase order. This expression applies separately to grid voltage \(\underline v_{\mathrm{s}}=v_\alpha+jv_\beta\), converter voltage \(\underline v_{\mathrm{conv}}=v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha}+jv_{\mathrm{conv}\beta}\), and current \(\underline i=i_\alpha+ji_\beta\). The sequence coefficients \(\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^\pm=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^\pm+jv_{\mathrm{s}q}^\pm\) represent the grid voltage in the corresponding synchronous frames. The voltage unbalance factor is \(u=|\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^-|/|\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+|\), provided that \(|\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+|>0\).

For \(s=+1\) or \(-1\), the positive or negative Park transformation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ch4-park}
\begin{bmatrix}x_d^s\\x_q^s\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&s\sin\theta\\-s\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_\alpha\\x_\beta\end{bmatrix},\quad
\underline x_{dq}^s=e^{-js\theta}\underline x.
\end{equation}
The inverse is \(\underline x=e^{js\theta}\underline x_{dq}^s\). In the positive frame, \eqref{eq:seq} becomes \(\underline X^++\underline X^-e^{-j2\theta}\); in the negative frame it becomes \(\underline X^-+\underline X^+e^{j2\theta}\). Hence each sequence is constant in its own frame and the opposite sequence appears at twice the fundamental frequency. This property underlies dual-frame current control \cite{song1999}.

Assume equal filter inductances \(L\), resistances \(R\), and averaged converter phase voltages. The phase equations, with current toward the rectifier, are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:abc}
L\frac{di_k}{dt}=v_k-Ri_k-v_{\mathrm{conv}k},\qquad k\in\{a,b,c\}.
\end{equation}
Applying \eqref{eq:ch4-clarke} gives the stationary-frame model
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model}
L\dot{\underline i}=\underline v_{\mathrm{s}}-R\underline i-\underline v_{\mathrm{conv}}.
\end{equation}
For either synchronous rotation \(s\omega\), differentiation of \(\underline i=e^{js\theta}\underline i_{dq}^s\) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dqmodel}
\begin{aligned}
L\dot i_d^s&=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^s-Ri_d^s-v_{\mathrm{conv}d}^s+s\omega L i_q^s,\\
L\dot i_q^s&=v_{\mathrm{s}q}^s-Ri_q^s-v_{\mathrm{conv}q}^s-s\omega L i_d^s.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The cross-coupling signs reverse in the negative frame. These equations apply to the complete signal in the selected frame or to the extracted corresponding sequence.

Define grid and converter-terminal powers as \(p_{\mathrm{in}}=\sum v_ki_k\) and \(p_{\mathrm{out}}=\sum v_{\mathrm{conv}k}i_k\). With \(W_L=\tfrac12L\sum i_k^2\), multiplication of \eqref{eq:abc} by current and summation gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy}
p_{\mathrm{in}}=p_{\mathrm{out}}+R\sum_{k=a,b,c}i_k^2+\frac{dW_L}{dt}.
\end{equation}
For an ideal converter, DC-link capacitance \(C\), voltage \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}\), and load power \(p_{\rm load}\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dc}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 CV_{\mathrm{dc}}^2\right)=p_{\mathrm{out}}-p_{\rm load}.
\end{equation}
Converter losses can be included as an additional subtraction. Equations \eqref{eq:energy} and \eqref{eq:dc} show why the grid input power and power supplied to the DC link cannot generally be treated as identical \cite{li2010journal}.

\section{Instantaneous active and reactive powers}
For a zero-sequence-free system, choose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pq}
p=\frac32(v_\alpha i_\alpha+v_\beta i_\beta),\qquad
q=\frac32(v_\beta i_\alpha-v_\alpha i_\beta).
\end{equation}
Thus \(p+jq=\tfrac32\underline v_{\mathrm{s}}\,\underline i^*\). The \(3/2\) factor results from amplitude-invariant Clarke scaling. In this convention, positive \(q\) corresponds to a current lagging positive-sequence voltage.

Let \(A=\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+\underline I^{+*}+\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^-\underline I^{-*}\), \(B=\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+\underline I^{-*}\) and \(D=\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^-\underline I^{+*}\). Substitution of \eqref{eq:seq} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complex}
p+jq=\frac32\left(A+Be^{j2\theta}+De^{-j2\theta}\right).
\end{equation}
Opposite-sequence products produce power oscillations at twice the grid frequency. The power waveforms are
\begin{equation}
p=P_0+P_c\cos2\theta+P_s\sin2\theta,\quad
q=Q_0+Q_c\cos2\theta+Q_s\sin2\theta.
\end{equation}
The coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coeff}
\begin{aligned}
P_0&=\tfrac32\Re A,& Q_0&=\tfrac32\Im A,\\
P_c&=\tfrac32\Re(B+D),& P_s&=-\tfrac32\Im(B-D),\\
Q_c&=\tfrac32\Im(B+D),& Q_s&=\tfrac32\Re(B-D).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These coefficients are explicit functions of positive- and negative-sequence \(dq\) components through the definitions of \(A\), \(B\) and \(D\). Additional voltage or current harmonics generate other power frequencies. The particular instantaneous reactive-power definition matters: Li et al. use an orthogonal time-delayed voltage in their unbalanced-grid formulation, so its ripple terms must not be silently equated with \eqref{eq:pq} \cite{li2010journal}.

For compact reference generation, define \(a_+=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+\), \(a_-=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-\), \(b_+=v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+\), and \(b_-=v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-\), where \(v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+\) and \(v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+\) are the positive-sequence grid voltage components and \(v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-\) and \(v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-\) are the negative-sequence components. Let \(\bm i=[i_d^+,i_q^+,i_d^-,i_q^-]^T\) be the current vector. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:matrix}
\begin{bmatrix}P_0\\Q_0\\P_c\\P_s\\Q_c\\Q_s\end{bmatrix}
=\frac32
\begin{bmatrix}
a_+&b_+&a_-&b_-\\
b_+&-a_+&b_-&-a_-\\
a_-&b_-&a_+&b_+\\
b_-&-a_-&-b_+&a_+\\
b_-&-a_-&b_+&-a_+\\
-a_-&-b_-&a_+&b_+
\end{bmatrix}\bm i.
\end{equation}

The four real current components cannot generally impose six independent power coefficients. Selecting four independent rows permits a solution when the resulting matrix is nonsingular, subject to current and converter-voltage constraints.

\section{DC-link ripple}
Suppose the net converter-terminal power has a small double-frequency component \(\widehat P_2\cos(2\omega t+\phi)\), with approximately constant load power. Write \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=V_0+\widetilde v\), where \(|\widetilde v|\ll V_0\). Linearizing \eqref{eq:dc} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ripple}
CV_0\frac{d\widetilde v}{dt}=\widehat P_2\cos(2\omega t+\phi),\qquad
\widehat V_2\simeq\frac{\widehat P_2}{2\omega CV_0}.
\end{equation}
The amplitudes are peak values; the ripple frequency is \(100\) Hz for a \(50\)-Hz supply and \(120\) Hz for a \(60\)-Hz supply. This approximation excludes switching ripple and assumes a nearly constant DC-side load. From \eqref{eq:energy}, eliminating grid active-power ripple does not necessarily eliminate converter-terminal power ripple, because filter magnetic energy and resistive loss may still oscillate.

\section{Current-reference objectives}
Four fundamental current components allow a choice such as prescribed \(P_0,Q_0\) and \(P_c=P_s=0\), prescribed \(P_0,Q_0\) and \(Q_c=Q_s=0\), or prescribed average powers with zero negative-sequence current. In practice the demands must also respect phase-current and converter-voltage limits.

For example, when \(Q_0=0\), \(\Delta=|\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+|^2-|\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^-|^2\ne0\), and \(P^\star\) is the desired average grid power, the references
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ipc}
\underline I^{+\star}=\frac{2P^\star}{3\Delta}\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^+,\qquad
\underline I^{-\star}=-\frac{2P^\star}{3\Delta}\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^-
\end{equation}
give \(P_0=P^\star\), \(Q_0=0\), and \(P_c=P_s=0\) in \eqref{eq:complex}. The denominator illustrates the growing current demand as the sequence magnitudes approach equality. Current limiting and power curtailment may then be necessary.

Alternatively, set \(\underline I^-=0\) to obtain balanced fundamental currents. For desired average powers \(P^\star,Q^\star\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balanced}
\underline I^{+\star}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{P^\star-jQ^\star}{\underline V_{\mathrm{s}}^{+*}}.
\end{equation}
This achieves the desired average powers but generally leaves both active- and reactive-power ripple because negative-sequence voltage interacts with positive-sequence current.

\section{Continuous-time power-flow analysis in stationary and \(dq\) frames}
\label{sec:li_wu_continuous}
This section rewrites the principal relationships in real-valued, component and matrix form expressing equations in the stationary-frame. Grid voltage is \(v\), current directed toward the converter is \(i\), and converter AC terminal voltage is \(v_{\mathrm{conv}}\). \(p_{\mathrm{in}}\) denotes grid-side power and \(p_{\mathrm{out}}\) denotes converter-terminal power. The output of the AFE rectifier is connected to the DC link, whereas the input terminal is on the grid side of the AC filter. The AC-filter resistance is \(R\), its inductance is \(L\), and \(\theta(t)=\omega t+\theta_0\) unless stated otherwise.

The relationships below are continuous-time averaged converter equations. Quantities evaluated from ideal switching states can contain components at the switching frequency. The fundamental-frequency power coefficients are obtained after adopting the stated averaged-waveform model. A fixed-frequency, sinusoidal steady state is assumed only where a constant sequence coefficient or quarter-cycle delay is explicitly introduced. The differential equations themselves do not require steady state.

\subsection{Three-phase and stationary-frame converter equations}
Let the upper-switch functions of the three bridge legs be \(S_a,S_b,S_c\). For an ideal two-level converter and a three-wire AC connection. The AC terminal voltages relative to the floating AC neutral are expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_switching_abc}
\begin{bmatrix}v_{\mathrm{conv}a}\\v_{\mathrm{conv}b}\\v_{\mathrm{conv}c}\end{bmatrix}
=\frac{V_{\mathrm{dc}}}{3}
\begin{bmatrix}
2&-1&-1\\-1&2&-1\\-1&-1&2
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}S_a\\S_b\\S_c\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The same equation can be used for the averaged switching functions in an averaged model. The AC-side continuous-time model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_dynamics_ab}
\begin{aligned}
L\frac{di_\alpha}{dt}&=v_\alpha-Ri_\alpha-v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha},\\
L\frac{di_\beta}{dt}&=v_\beta-Ri_\beta-v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta},\\
v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha}&=V_{\mathrm{dc}}s_\alpha,\qquad
v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta}=V_{\mathrm{dc}}s_\beta .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here \(s_\alpha,s_\beta\) are the amplitude-invariant Clarke components of the three switching functions. Their relationship to the phase variables follows from \eqref{eq:ch4-clarke}; no zero-sequence current is present in the ideal three-wire model. If the converter is commanded with averaged switching functions, \eqref{eq:li_dynamics_ab} describes the fundamental and low-frequency behavior rather than individual switching transitions.

The DC-link current equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_dc_current}
C\frac{dV_{\mathrm{dc}}}{dt}
=i_{\rm rec}-i_{\rm load},\qquad
i_{\rm rec}=\frac32\left(s_\alpha i_\alpha+s_\beta i_\beta\right).
\end{equation}
The direction of \(i_{\rm rec}\) is positive into the DC-link node. Multiplying \eqref{eq:li_dc_current} by \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}\) yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_dc_power_ct}
\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac12CV_{\mathrm{dc}}^2\right)
=\frac32\left(v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha} i_\alpha+v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta} i_\beta\right)
-V_{\mathrm{dc}}i_{\rm load}.
\end{equation}
The bridge is assumed lossless in \eqref{eq:li_dc_power_ct}. The formula remains valid for a time-varying \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}\), provided \(v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha}=V_{\mathrm{dc}}s_\alpha\) and \(v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta}=V_{\mathrm{dc}}s_\beta\) are evaluated at the same instant. It makes clear that a constant converter power alone does not produce constant voltage if the load power varies at the same time.

\subsection{Positive- and negative-sequence \(dq\) dynamics}
For a steady fundamental voltage and current, write the individual sequence components in real form. For any stationary-frame vector \((x_\alpha,x_\beta)\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_real_sequence}
\begin{aligned}
x_\alpha={}&x_d^+\cos\theta-x_q^+\sin\theta
+x_d^-\cos\theta+x_q^-\sin\theta,\\
x_\beta={}&x_d^+\sin\theta+x_q^+\cos\theta
-x_d^-\sin\theta+x_q^-\cos\theta .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The coefficients \(x_d^+,x_q^+\) are expressed in the positive synchronous frame and \(x_d^-,x_q^-\) in the negative synchronous frame. Equation \eqref{eq:li_real_sequence} applies separately to grid voltage \(v\), current \(i\), converter voltage \(v_{\mathrm{conv}}\), and the averaged switching vector \(s\), provided the corresponding fundamental sequence coefficients are used. It is equivalent to the compact complex representation in \eqref{eq:seq} but shows every sine and cosine explicitly.

The sequence current dynamics are obtained from \eqref{eq:li_dynamics_ab} by the transformations in \eqref{eq:ch4-park}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_dq_expanded}
\begin{aligned}
L\frac{di_d^+}{dt}&=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+-Ri_d^+-v_{\mathrm{conv}d}^++\omega Li_q^+,&
L\frac{di_q^+}{dt}&=v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+-Ri_q^+-v_{\mathrm{conv}q}^+-\omega Li_d^+,\\
L\frac{di_d^-}{dt}&=v_{\mathrm{s}d}^--Ri_d^--v_{\mathrm{conv}d}^--\omega Li_q^-,&
L\frac{di_q^-}{dt}&=v_{\mathrm{s}q}^--Ri_q^--v_{\mathrm{conv}q}^-+\omega Li_d^- .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The opposite signs of the cross-coupling terms follow from opposite frame rotation. The equations describe the dynamics of separately identified sequence components; expressing the total measured current in either frame still includes the opposite-sequence \(2\omega\) term. If the grid frequency changes, \(\omega\) in the frame equations is replaced by \(d\theta/dt\), and the validity of any fixed \(T/4\) delay must be reconsidered.

\subsection{Grid active and reactive powers in \(dq\) variables}
With amplitude-invariant scaling, the grid-side power definitions in \eqref{eq:pq} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_pq_real}
p_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac32(v_\alpha i_\alpha+v_\beta i_\beta),\qquad
q_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac32(v_\beta i_\alpha-v_\alpha i_\beta).
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:li_real_sequence} for the voltage and current gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_grid_pq_ripple}
\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm{in}}&=P_{\mathrm{in},0}+P_{\mathrm{in},c}\cos2\theta+P_{\mathrm{in},s}\sin2\theta,\\
q_{\mathrm{in}}&=Q_{\mathrm{in},0}+Q_{\mathrm{in},c}\cos2\theta+Q_{\mathrm{in},s}\sin2\theta .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The six coefficients can be written directly in \(dq\) components:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_grid_P_real}
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{in},0}&=\frac32(v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_d^++v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_q^+
                  +v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_d^-+v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_q^-),\\
P_{\mathrm{in},c}&=\frac32(v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_d^++v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_q^+
                  +v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_d^-+v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_q^-),\\
P_{\mathrm{in},s}&=\frac32(v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_d^+-v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_q^+
                  -v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_d^-+v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_q^-).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(P_{\mathrm{in},0}\) contains products of voltage and current from the same sequence. The coefficients \(P_{\mathrm{in},c}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{in},s}\) contain cross-sequence products. The latter therefore disappear for balanced positive-sequence voltage and current, but can persist if either the negative-sequence voltage or current is nonzero.

Using the cross-product reactive-power convention in \eqref{eq:li_pq_real}, the reactive coefficients are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_grid_Q_real}
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\mathrm{in},0}&=\frac32(v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_d^+-v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_q^+
                  +v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_d^--v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_q^-),\\
Q_{\mathrm{in},c}&=\frac32(v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_d^+-v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_q^+
                  +v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_d^--v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_q^-),\\
Q_{\mathrm{in},s}&=\frac32(-v_{\mathrm{s}d}^-i_d^+-v_{\mathrm{s}q}^-i_q^+
                  +v_{\mathrm{s}d}^+i_d^-+v_{\mathrm{s}q}^+i_q^-).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These equations are the expanded real form of \eqref{eq:matrix}. They do not add independent control degrees of freedom: the four fundamental current variables remain \(i_d^+,i_q^+,i_d^-,i_q^-\). With two prescribed average powers, only two further independent scalar constraints can generally be imposed without changing topology or adding other current components.

\subsection{Filter and converter-terminal power components}
it is emphasized that the instantaneous input, filter and output powers must satisfy the same energy balance \cite{wu2008}. In stationary coordinates the resistance and inductor powers are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_filter_ct}
\begin{aligned}
p_R&=\frac32R(i_\alpha^2+i_\beta^2),\\
p_L&=\frac32L\left(i_\alpha\frac{di_\alpha}{dt}
                      +i_\beta\frac{di_\beta}{dt}\right)
     =\frac{dW_L}{dt},\\
p_{\mathrm{out}}&=p_{\mathrm{in}}-p_R-p_L .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These are continuous-time identities for equal \(R,L\), including transients and current harmonics. The instantaneous inductor power can have either sign because the inductor alternately stores and releases energy; it should not be treated as a dissipative loss.

For fixed fundamental sequence currents, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_hj}
\begin{aligned}
S_i&=(i_d^+)^2+(i_q^+)^2+(i_d^-)^2+(i_q^-)^2,\\
H&=i_d^+i_d^-+i_q^+i_q^-,\qquad
J=i_q^+i_d^--i_d^+i_q^- .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Direct expansion of \eqref{eq:li_real_sequence} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_filter_2w}
\begin{aligned}
p_R&=\frac32RS_i+3R(H\cos2\theta-J\sin2\theta),\\
p_L&=-3\omega L(H\sin2\theta+J\cos2\theta).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If either current sequence is zero, \(H=J=0\), so the ideal fundamental-current contribution to \(p_L\) is zero and \(p_R\) is constant. With both sequences present, their interaction creates the double-frequency filter-power terms in \eqref{eq:li_wu_filter_2w}. This is an important distinction from the grid-power oscillation, which also depends on the negative-sequence voltage.

Writing \(p_{\mathrm{out}}=P_{\mathrm{out},0}+P_{\mathrm{out},c}\cos2\theta+P_{\mathrm{out},s}\sin2\theta\), substitution into \eqref{eq:li_wu_filter_ct} yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_output_coeff}
\begin{aligned}
P_{\mathrm{out},0}&=P_{\mathrm{in},0}-\frac32RS_i,\\
P_{\mathrm{out},c}&=P_{\mathrm{in},c}-3RH+3\omega LJ,\\
P_{\mathrm{out},s}&=P_{\mathrm{in},s}+3RJ+3\omega LH .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
These three equations connect the input-power coefficients to the converter-terminal power and show explicitly why \(P_{\mathrm{in},c}=P_{\mathrm{in},s}=0\) does not generally imply \(P_{\mathrm{out},c}=P_{\mathrm{out},s}=0\). Conversely, some control strategies can enforce \(P_{\mathrm{out},c}=P_{\mathrm{out},s}=0\) by allowing nonzero input-power coefficients. The associated converter-power ripple amplitude is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:li_wu_output_amplitude}
\widehat P_{\mathrm{out},2}=\sqrt{P_{\mathrm{out},c}^{\,2}+P_{\mathrm{out},s}^{\,2}}.
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:li_wu_filter_2w}--\eqref{eq:li_wu_output_amplitude} assume constant fundamental sequence-current coefficients. During transients, \eqref{eq:li_wu_filter_ct} remains the appropriate starting point because derivatives of the changing sequence components contribute to the inductor power.

\subsection{DC-link voltage: exact differential equation and harmonic response}
Under the assumption of a resistive DC load, it is shown how oscillating converter output current leads to DC-link voltage ripple \cite{wu2008}. For a load resistance \(R_l\), define \(G=1/R_l\). Equation \eqref{eq:li_dc_current} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wu_resistive_ode}
C\frac{dV_{\mathrm{dc}}}{dt}+GV_{\mathrm{dc}}=i_{\rm rec}(t).
\end{equation}
This equation is exact for a constant resistor and a prescribed rectifier output current. Its solution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wu_resistive_solution}
V_{\mathrm{dc}}(t)=V_{\mathrm{dc}}(0)e^{-Gt/C}
+\frac1C\int_0^t e^{-G(t-\tau)/C}i_{\rm rec}(\tau)\,d\tau .
\end{equation}
The first term is the natural response. The second term shows the filtering of the current supplied by the bridge. In a closed-loop rectifier, \(i_{\rm rec}\) depends on the AC current, switching command, and DC voltage; the expression is not an assertion that this current is independent of the rest of the system.

For an illustrative steady periodic current component at angular frequency \(\Omega\), let
\begin{equation}
i_{\rm rec}=I_0+I_c\cos\Omega t+I_s\sin\Omega t .
\end{equation}
After the natural response has decayed, \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}=V_0+V_c\cos\Omega t+V_s\sin\Omega t\), where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wu_voltage_coefficients}
\begin{aligned}
V_0&=\frac{I_0}{G},\\
V_c&=\frac{GI_c-\Omega C I_s}{G^2+(\Omega C)^2},\\
V_s&=\frac{\Omega C I_c+GI_s}{G^2+(\Omega C)^2},\\
\widehat V_\Omega&=\frac{\sqrt{I_c^2+I_s^2}}{\sqrt{G^2+(\Omega C)^2}} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
At \(\Omega=2\omega\), these equations describe the second-harmonic current contribution to the DC-link voltage for the specified resistive load. The load conductance affects both magnitude and phase. The simpler power-ripple approximation in \eqref{eq:ripple} neglects that load response and is valid under its separately stated constant-load-power and small-ripple assumptions; it must not be substituted for \eqref{eq:wu_voltage_coefficients} without checking the load model.

More generally, if the output current has components at \(2\omega,4\omega,\ldots\), each component can be inserted into \eqref{eq:wu_voltage_coefficients} using its own \(\Omega\), and the resulting voltage components superposed because \eqref{eq:wu_resistive_ode} is linear in \(V_{\mathrm{dc}}\) for a prescribed current. The bridge output power, however, is the product \(p_{\mathrm{out}}=V_{\mathrm{dc}}i_{\rm rec}\). Multiplying two waveforms containing DC, \(2\omega\), and \(4\omega\) terms can generate DC and components at \(2\omega,4\omega,6\omega\), and \(8\omega\). This product explains the higher even-order power terms and their presence depends on the harmonic content and operating conditions and is not implied by a model containing only constant fundamental sequence coefficients.

\subsection{AC current harmonics and their coupling to power}
The fundamental-sequence model gives a particularly compact \(0\) and \(2\omega\) power decomposition. To include current harmonics without assuming a particular controller, write the stationary current as a sum of rotating components
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wu_harmonic_general}
\begin{aligned}
i_\alpha(t)&=\sum_h I_h\cos(\sigma_h h\omega t+\phi_h),\\
i_\beta(t)&=\sum_h I_h\sin(\sigma_h h\omega t+\phi_h),
\qquad \sigma_h\in\{-1,+1\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here \(h\) is a positive harmonic order, \(\sigma_h\) describes its rotation direction, and \(I_h\) is a peak space-vector magnitude. A fundamental voltage interacting with a current component of order \(h\) can produce power components at the corresponding sum or difference of their signed angular frequencies. Thus a third-harmonic current can contribute \(2\omega\) or \(4\omega\) terms depending on its sequence, while higher odd-order currents can produce higher even-order power terms. Interactions among multiple current harmonics also affect \(p_R\) and \(p_L\) through the quadratic expressions in \eqref{eq:li_wu_filter_ct}.

In a conventional three-wire system, zero-sequence line current cannot flow. A triplen harmonic that is identical in all three phase currents is therefore excluded; any third-harmonic component considered in a three-wire analysis must be compatible with the zero-sum phase-current constraint. The existence and magnitude of a third- or higher-order current are properties of the converter and closed-loop response, not unavoidable consequences of voltage unbalance alone.

\subsection{Modeling and Simulations}
This section validates the complete drive model and controllers through a focused suite of studies: (i) steady-state operation to verify ripple, losses, and harmonic content; (ii) low-speed operation to probe back-EMF limits and non-idealities; (iii)  dynamic performance under speed/current steps; (iv) parameter variation to gauge robustness against model mismatch; and (v) load variation to assess torque disturbance rejection.
\subsubsection{Steady State Operation}
With an ideal plant/inverter (no non-linearities or disturbances), the modulated MPC (SVM-based) holds 1000 rpm with very low harmonic content across loads: at low load (10\% rated torque, \(0.3~\mathrm{N\,m}\)) the phase-current THD is 0.4\% (see Fig.~\ref{fig:M2PCIdeal_1000rpm_low}), and at medium load (50\% rated torque, \(1.75~\mathrm{N\,m}\)) it is 0.7\% (see Fig.~\ref{fig:M2PCIdeal_1000rpm_med}). The improved spectral cleanliness stems from optimizing a continuous average voltage each sample and realizing it via SVM at fixed switching frequency, which suppresses staircase ripple. In this ideal setting the modulated MPC delivers robust steady-state operation with lower THD than PI at the same speed/load and far below FCS, while maintaining tight speed regulation.

\subsubsection{Low Speed Operation}

\subsubsection{Parameter Variation}
At 5\% rated speed (100~rpm), using a biased prediction model (\(R_s{+}25\%\), \(L_{d,q}{-}25\%\)) leaves the modulated MPC’s steady-state quality unchanged: phase-current THD stays at 0.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:M2PCParamVariation}). The fixed-frequency, SVM-based actuation and per-sample voltage optimization make the controller largely insensitive to moderate \(R\!-\!L\) errors—more robust than PI (1.3\% \(\rightarrow\) 1.7\%) and far better than FCS (32\% \(\rightarrow\) 41\%).
