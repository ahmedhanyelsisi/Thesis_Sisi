\chapter{Proposed IOPC-Integrated Modulated Predictive Current Control in the Stationary Reference Frame}
\label{ch:proposed-iopc-mmpcc}

\section{Introduction}
The preceding chapters established the mathematical model of the three-phase active front-end (AFE) rectifier, its conventional control, and the principles of finite-control-set and modulated model predictive control. The modulated approach introduced in Chapter~3 selects two adjacent active voltage vectors and a zero vector, allowing the converter to apply a controlled average voltage during each modulation period. This methodology is extended here through an independent formulation in the stationary $\alpha\beta$ reference frame. Chapter~4 provides the underlying stationary-frame plant model and the power-flow relationships under unbalanced grid voltage.

Under voltage unbalance, satisfactory current tracking alone does not determine the power delivered to the DC link. The reference currents must account for the interaction between the grid voltage, converter-terminal voltage, and filter energy. In particular, eliminating the oscillation of grid-side active power does not generally eliminate the oscillation of converter-terminal active power. The difference follows from the resistance loss and time-varying energy of the AC filter. Consequently, a predictive controller requires a reference-generation stage that explicitly selects the intended power objectives.

This chapter develops an integrated input-output power control and modulated model predictive current control (IOPC--MMPCC) architecture. IOPC specifies the stationary-frame current references using the power conditions developed by Li et al.~\cite{li2010journal}. MMPCC then tracks these references by predicting the grid currents, optimizing the duty cycles of each adjacent vector pair, and selecting the feasible combination with the smallest predicted current error. The complete proposed control law operates in $\alpha\beta$ coordinates. No Park transformation or separate positive- and negative-sequence rotating-frame current regulator is required.

The contribution is the explicit integration of a published IOPC reference generator with a stationary-frame, delay-compensated, constrained modulated predictive current regulator. The IOPC equations themselves are not claimed as new. The development also establishes causal converter-voltage feedback, future-reference evaluation, duty-cycle feasibility, and the limits of the resulting power objectives. It is a theoretical controller design; neither experimental validation nor a general claim of novelty relative to all published predictive controllers is made.

The chapter first reviews alternative control strategies and compares IPC, OPC, and IOPC. It then develops the IOPC reference equations, describes the integrated architecture, derives the predictive model and constrained modulation problem, and presents the complete algorithm. Practical limitations and the conditions requiring subsequent validation conclude the development.

\section{Review of Control Strategies Under Unbalanced Grid Conditions}
\label{sec:ch5-review}
The broader development of three-phase PWM rectifiers is reviewed in \cite{ch5-singh2004,ch5-rodriguez2005}. For the unbalanced-grid problem, the following review follows the classification in Li et al.~\cite{li2010journal}. The original publications are cited to identify the relevant contributions. Where an original work has not been inspected independently, the discussion is explicitly based on Li et al.'s account; the accompanying audit records the source-access status. This distinction prevents a secondary literature summary from being presented as an independent verification of every cited method.

\subsection{Dual Positive- and Negative-Sequence Control}
A fundamental unbalanced voltage contains components with opposite directions of rotation. A controller can represent each component in its corresponding synchronous frame, where the selected sequence becomes constant while the opposite sequence appears at twice the fundamental frequency. Two PI current regulators can then control the extracted components. The dual-current scheme of Song and Nam~\cite{song1999} uses this principle, with filtering to separate the two sequence currents. Rioual et al.~\cite{ch5-rioual1996} provide an earlier generalized unbalanced-network formulation; their role in this development is discussed by Li et al.~\cite{li2010journal}.

The resulting structure offers explicit control of the sequence currents, but it requires transformations, sequence separation, and coordination of the current references. Filters that reject the opposite-sequence component introduce dynamics and affect the transient response. The processing burden depends on the extraction method and implementation; the use of two frames does not itself imply an impractical controller. Li et al. also describe coordinated converter control by Xu~\cite{ch5-xu2008}, in which the positive-frame main controller is supplemented by a negative-frame controller. These approaches provide useful background, but their rotating frames are not adopted in the proposed implementation.

\subsection{Stationary-Frame Resonant Control}
A conventional PI regulator has finite gain at the nonzero fundamental frequency of stationary-frame current references. Its integral action therefore does not, by itself, guarantee zero sinusoidal tracking error. A proportional-resonant (PR) regulator introduces the corresponding internal model at the desired frequency. The ideal form used in Li et al.~\cite{li2010journal} is
\begin{equation}
G_{\mathrm{PR}}(s)=K_{p,c}+K_r\frac{s}{s^2+\omega_s^2},
\label{eq:ch5-pr}
\end{equation}
where $K_{p,c}$ is the proportional gain, $K_r$ is the resonant gain, and $\omega_s$ is the fundamental angular frequency. The ideal resonant term has unbounded gain at $\omega_s$; practical implementations must consider damping, frequency mismatch, discretization, and closed-loop stability.

Zmood and Holmes~\cite{ch5-zmood2003} establish the connection between stationary resonant regulation and synchronous PI regulation. Additional resonant branches can target selected harmonics, increasing the number of controller states and the need to check stability. Li et al. discuss frequency-domain regulation and multiple-harmonic compensation in \cite{ch5-zmood2001,ch5-bojoi2005,ch5-liserre2006,ch5-hwang2006}, together with unbalanced-grid applications in \cite{ch5-hwang2007,ch5-roiu2008,ch5-etxeberria2007,ch5-hu2009}. The later journal treatment by Roiu et al.~\cite{ch5-roiu2010} also implements stationary-frame current-reference generation and resonant current regulation.

These methods show that unbalance does not require rotating-frame current controllers. In the present architecture, the inner resonant current regulator is replaced by a predictive regulator. Resonant signal processing may still be used to estimate fundamental and quadrature voltages; this signal-processing role is distinct from closed-loop current regulation.

\subsection{Instantaneous Power-Based Control}
The power-control objective and the current-regulation method are separate design choices. IPC, OPC, and IOPC specify different power conditions, while PI, PR, or predictive controllers may be used to track the resulting current references. With current directed from the grid toward the converter, Chapter~4, \eqref{eq:energy}, gives
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{in}}=p_{\mathrm{out}}+p_R+p_L,\qquad
p_R=\frac32R\lVert\mathbf i\rVert^2,\quad
p_L=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac34L\lVert\mathbf i\rVert^2\right).
\label{eq:ch5-power-balance}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf i=[i_\alpha\ i_\beta]^T$, $p_{\mathrm{in}}$ is measured before the AC filter, and $p_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the power at the converter AC terminals. For an ideal bridge, $p_{\mathrm{out}}$ enters the DC link. The inductor term represents reversible energy exchange, not dissipative loss. Both filter terms can contain a double-frequency component.

The reactive quantities used in the following IPC/OPC/IOPC comparison follow Li et al.'s quadrature-voltage convention. Their relation to Chapter~4's cross-product convention is established in Section~\ref{sec:ch5-iopc}. A bar denotes the fundamental steady-state average, and a tilde denotes the oscillating part.

\subsubsection{Input-Power Control}
The IPC conditions are
\begin{equation}
\overline p_{\mathrm{in}}=P_{\mathrm{ref}},\qquad
\overline q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}=0,\qquad
\widetilde p_{\mathrm{in}}=0.
\label{eq:ch5-ipc-objectives}
\end{equation}
They regulate average input power and suppress its fundamental double-frequency oscillation. Li et al. associate this category with \cite{ch5-rioual1996,song1999}. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-power-balance} nevertheless gives $\widetilde p_{\mathrm{out}}=-\widetilde p_R-\widetilde p_L$ when $\widetilde p_{\mathrm{in}}=0$. The filter can therefore transfer oscillating power to the DC side even when the grid-side power is constant. IPC alone does not ensure a ripple-free DC-link voltage.

\subsubsection{Output-Power Control}
OPC instead imposes
\begin{equation}
\overline p_{\mathrm{out}}=P_{\mathrm{ref}},\qquad
\overline q_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm Q}=0,\qquad
\widetilde p_{\mathrm{out}}=0.
\label{eq:ch5-opc-objectives}
\end{equation}
The output-power strategy of Yin et al.~\cite{ch5-yin2008} is discussed in Li et al.~\cite{li2010journal}. Regulating converter-terminal power directly addresses the low-frequency energy entering the DC link. For constant load power and negligible bridge losses, suppression of its double-frequency oscillation removes the corresponding forced DC-voltage ripple. Load changes, switching ripple, and nonidealities remain separate effects. Zero average output reactive power does not impose zero average input reactive power; the filter separates the two terminals.

\subsubsection{Input-Output Power Control}
IOPC combines the input average-power conditions with output ripple suppression:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\overline p_{\mathrm{in}}=P_{\mathrm{ref}},\qquad
\overline q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}=0,\qquad
\widetilde p_{\mathrm{out}}=0.}
\label{eq:ch5-iopc-objectives}
\end{equation}
The IOPC literature identified by Li et al. includes synchronous, hybrid, and stationary implementations~\cite{ch5-suh2002pesc,ch5-suh2002ias,ch5-suh2006hybrid,ch5-suh2006model,wu2008,ch5-wu2008repetitive,ch5-stankovic2001}. Their common motivation is to account for filter power when controlling the DC-side energy transfer. The proposed chapter uses Li et al.'s stationary formulation rather than reconstructing these other implementations.

The input power is permitted to oscillate as required to supply the filter energy variation while maintaining constant converter-terminal power. Li et al. describe the zero-average-reactive condition as unity power factor in an average sense. Under unbalance this wording must be interpreted narrowly: $\overline q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}=0$ does not prove unity true three-phase power factor, balanced currents, or zero instantaneous reactive power. It also need not imply zero mean cross-product reactive power under the convention used in Chapter~4.

IOPC is selected because its objectives address average grid power and the principal low-frequency source of DC-link ripple together. Its additional dependence on converter-terminal voltages makes reference calculation and implementation more involved. Its suitability remains conditional on matrix conditioning, current limits, and the available converter voltage.

\section{Mathematical Formulation of IOPC in the Stationary Frame}
\label{sec:ch5-iopc}
\subsection{Notation, Scaling, and Instantaneous Powers}
Chapter~4 uses $v_{\mathrm{s}\alpha},v_{\mathrm{s}\beta}$ for grid quantities and $v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha},v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta}$ for converter quantities. To reproduce the original paper transparently, this chapter uses the shorter aliases
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e=\begin{bmatrix}e_\alpha\\e_\beta\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\mathrm{s}\alpha}\\v_{\mathrm{s}\beta}\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf v=\begin{bmatrix}v_\alpha\\v_\beta\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}v_{\mathrm{conv}\alpha}\\v_{\mathrm{conv}\beta}\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:ch5-aliases}
\end{equation}
Thus an unsubscripted $v_\alpha$ in this chapter denotes converter voltage, not grid voltage. The amplitude-invariant Clarke map in Chapter~4, \eqref{eq:ch4-clarke}, is retained:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf x_{\alpha\beta}=\mathbf T_{\mathrm C}\mathbf x_{abc},\qquad
\mathbf T_{\mathrm C}=\frac23
\begin{bmatrix}1&-1/2&-1/2\\0&\sqrt3/2&-\sqrt3/2\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:ch5-clarke}
\end{equation}
The converter is three-phase and three-wire, so $i_a+i_b+i_c=0$. Equal phase-filter inductances and resistances are assumed. The power scaling is consequently $3/2$, rather than unity.

For a fundamental sinusoid $x(t)=X\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, define its lagging quadrature as
\begin{equation}
x'(t)=x\!\left(t-\frac{\pi}{2\omega}\right)=X\sin(\omega t+\phi),\qquad
\dot x=-\omega x',\quad \dot x'=\omega x.
\label{eq:ch5-quadrature-definition}
\end{equation}
A prime is a quadrature operator, not a time derivative. These identities assume constant frequency and fixed sinusoidal coefficients. They are not exact identities during amplitude steps or arbitrary waveform distortion.

The powers corresponding to Li et al.'s equations (9)--(12) are
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{in}}&=\frac32\mathbf e^T\mathbf i,&
q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}&=\frac32\mathbf e'^T\mathbf i,
\label{eq:ch5-input-powers}\\
p_{\mathrm{out}}&=\frac32\mathbf v^T\mathbf i,&
q_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm Q}&=\frac32\mathbf v'^T\mathbf i.
\label{eq:ch5-output-powers}
\end{align}
In contrast, the cross-product quantity in Chapter~4, \eqref{eq:pq}, is
\begin{equation}
q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\times}=\frac32(e_\beta i_\alpha-e_\alpha i_\beta).
\label{eq:ch5-cross-q}
\end{equation}
For a balanced positive-sequence voltage with $e_\alpha=E\cos\omega t$ and $e_\beta=E\sin\omega t$, $\mathbf e'=[e_\beta\ -e_\alpha]^T$, so the definitions coincide. For a negative-sequence voltage the quadrature vector has the opposite relationship. With both sequences present, neither the instantaneous quantities nor their averages can be silently interchanged. Superscript $\mathrm Q$ therefore identifies every reactive-power objective inherited from Li et al.

\subsection{Average and Oscillating Power Components}
For two fundamental scalar sinusoids $x,y$, the identities
\begin{equation}
xy+x'y'=XY\cos(\phi_x-\phi_y),\qquad
xy-x'y'=XY\cos(2\omega t+\phi_x+\phi_y)
\label{eq:ch5-products}
\end{equation}
separate the constant and double-frequency terms. Applying them componentwise gives
\begin{align}
\overline p_{\mathrm{in}}&=\frac34(\mathbf e^T\mathbf i+\mathbf e'^T\mathbf i'),&
\widetilde p_{\mathrm{in}}&=\frac34(\mathbf e^T\mathbf i-\mathbf e'^T\mathbf i'),
\label{eq:ch5-input-decomposition}\\
\overline q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}&=\frac34(\mathbf e'^T\mathbf i-\mathbf e^T\mathbf i'),&
\widetilde q_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm Q}&=\frac34(\mathbf e'^T\mathbf i+\mathbf e^T\mathbf i'),
\label{eq:ch5-q-decomposition}\\
\overline p_{\mathrm{out}}&=\frac34(\mathbf v^T\mathbf i+\mathbf v'^T\mathbf i'),&
\widetilde p_{\mathrm{out}}&=\frac34(\mathbf v^T\mathbf i-\mathbf v'^T\mathbf i').
\label{eq:ch5-output-decomposition}
\end{align}
The factors $3/4$ result from the $3/2$ Clarke power factor and the half-angle product identity. These expressions reproduce the content of Li et al.'s equations (14), (16), and (17), using a compact vector notation.

For output-ripple cancellation define
\begin{equation}
\kappa_1=\mathbf v^T\mathbf i-\mathbf v'^T\mathbf i',\qquad
\kappa_2=\mathbf v'^T\mathbf i+\mathbf v^T\mathbf i'.
\label{eq:ch5-kappa}
\end{equation}
For fixed fundamental coefficients, $\kappa_1$ and $\kappa_2$ are the cosine and sine quadratures of the same double-frequency waveform. Their simultaneous vanishing sets both independent ripple coefficients to zero. This is the stationary-frame counterpart of the double-frequency power analysis in Chapter~4; no sequence extraction is needed to evaluate it. Higher harmonics, estimator transients, and time-varying coefficients fall outside this two-coefficient description.

\subsection{Power-Condition Matrix and Its Solution}
Combining \eqref{eq:ch5-iopc-objectives} with \eqref{eq:ch5-input-decomposition}--\eqref{eq:ch5-kappa} gives the original IOPC matrix:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}P_{\mathrm{ref}}\\0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}
=\frac34\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
e_\alpha&e_\beta&e'_\alpha&e'_\beta\\
e'_\alpha&e'_\beta&-e_\alpha&-e_\beta\\
v_\alpha&v_\beta&-v'_\alpha&-v'_\beta\\
v'_\alpha&v'_\beta&v_\alpha&v_\beta
\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf M}
\begin{bmatrix}i_\alpha^\ast\\i_\beta^\ast\\i_\alpha^{\prime\ast}\\i_\beta^{\prime\ast}\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:ch5-iopc-matrix}
\end{equation}
The first row specifies mean input active power. The second sets mean input quadrature reactive power to zero. The third and fourth remove the two output active-power ripple coefficients. The four unknowns are the two current components and their fundamental quadratures, corresponding to four real sinusoidal coefficients rather than four independently measured currents.

When $\det\mathbf M\ne0$, the mathematical solution is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf z^\ast=\frac{4P_{\mathrm{ref}}}{3}\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf e_1,\qquad
\mathbf z^\ast=[i_\alpha^\ast\ i_\beta^\ast\ i_\alpha^{\prime\ast}\ i_\beta^{\prime\ast}]^T,
\quad \mathbf e_1=[1\ 0\ 0\ 0]^T.
\label{eq:ch5-matrix-solution}
\end{equation}
In software, a pivoted linear solve or QR factorization is preferable to explicitly forming an inverse. Expanding the determinant gives
\begin{align}
D_{\mathrm{IOPC}}={}&(e_\alpha^2+e_\alpha'^2)(v_\beta^2+v_\beta'^2)
 +(e_\beta^2+e_\beta'^2)(v_\alpha^2+v_\alpha'^2)\notag\\
&+2(v_\alpha v'_\beta-v'_\alpha v_\beta)
       (e_\alpha e'_\beta-e'_\alpha e_\beta)\notag\\
&-2(e_\alpha e_\beta+e'_\alpha e'_\beta)
       (v_\alpha v_\beta+v'_\alpha v'_\beta)
=\det\mathbf M.
\label{eq:ch5-iopc-denominator}
\end{align}
The first two cofactors in the first row are
\begin{align}
N_\alpha={}&(e'_\beta v'_\beta-e_\beta v_\beta)v_\alpha
 -(e_\beta v'_\beta+e'_\beta v_\beta)v'_\alpha
 +(v_\beta^2+v_\beta'^2)e_\alpha,
\label{eq:ch5-nalpha}\\
N_\beta={}&(e'_\alpha v'_\alpha-e_\alpha v_\alpha)v_\beta
 -(e_\alpha v'_\alpha+e'_\alpha v_\alpha)v'_\beta
 +(v_\alpha^2+v_\alpha'^2)e_\beta.
\label{eq:ch5-nbeta}
\end{align}
Cramer's rule therefore yields
\begin{equation}
\boxed{i_\alpha^\ast=\frac{4P_{\mathrm{ref}}N_\alpha}{3D_{\mathrm{IOPC}}},\qquad
i_\beta^\ast=\frac{4P_{\mathrm{ref}}N_\beta}{3D_{\mathrm{IOPC}}}.}
\label{eq:ch5-explicit-references}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:ch5-iopc-matrix}, \eqref{eq:ch5-iopc-denominator}, and \eqref{eq:ch5-explicit-references} are Li et al.'s equations (24)--(26), with current-reference notation made explicit. The numerator cofactors have units of voltage cubed and the determinant voltage to the fourth power; multiplying their ratio by power gives current, as required.

An independent factorization useful for checking the determinant is
\begin{align}
D_{\mathrm{IOPC}}&=U^2+Z^2,\notag\\
U&=e_\alpha v_\beta-e_\beta v_\alpha+e'_\alpha v'_\beta-e'_\beta v'_\alpha,\notag\\
Z&=e'_\alpha v_\beta-e'_\beta v_\alpha-e_\alpha v'_\beta+e_\beta v'_\alpha.
\label{eq:ch5-det-factorization}
\end{align}
For real variables, singularity is equivalent to $U=Z=0$. The determinant can become small relative to the signal scales during severe dips or unfavorable voltage relationships. A nonzero determinant alone is not an adequate numerical test: row scaling and a condition estimate are needed. As a limiting check, when $\mathbf v=\mathbf e$ the determinant becomes $4(e_\alpha e'_\beta-e'_\alpha e_\beta)^2$. It vanishes when the voltage trajectory collapses to a line, illustrating the loss of independent control information.

The matrix solution assumes the converter voltage is known. In the actual closed loop, converter voltage and current are coupled by the filter model, so the reference solve is not an independent proof that every requested operating point exists. Section~\ref{sec:ch5-architecture} supplies a causal estimate; convergence to the intended steady state remains a closed-loop question.

\subsection{Quadrature Variables and Converter-Voltage Estimation}
For a fixed fundamental waveform, applying the lagging quadrature operation to $\mathbf e=R\mathbf i+L\dot{\mathbf i}+\mathbf v$ gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf v'=\mathbf e'-R\mathbf i'-\omega L\mathbf i.
\label{eq:ch5-resistive-quadrature}
\end{equation}
This follows because the quadrature of $\dot{\mathbf i}$ is $\omega\mathbf i$. Neglecting $R$ recovers Li et al.'s equation (28):
\begin{equation}
v'_\alpha\simeq e'_\alpha-\omega L i_\alpha,\qquad
v'_\beta\simeq e'_\beta-\omega L i_\beta.
\label{eq:ch5-li-substitution}
\end{equation}
The approximation removes the need to extract delayed current or converter-voltage variables in the original implementation. It is a fundamental steady-state substitution, not an exact transient model. Retaining $R$ requires $\mathbf i'$ as well, and neither expression justifies applying the fundamental identity to raw switching ripple.

A stored quarter-period delay is straightforward at a fixed frequency when the sampling ratio is suitable. Fractional delays are needed otherwise. It also delays disturbance information and acts differently on harmonics, so its output is not automatically the quadrature of the fundamental alone. Resonant-based extraction can provide an in-phase fundamental estimate and its lagging quadrature, at the cost of estimator settling dynamics and sensitivity to frequency tuning.

For the proposed implementation, paired stationary signal generators are used on the measured grid voltage and reconstructed converter average voltage. A concrete continuous-time signal generator for each scalar input $x$ is
\begin{align}
\dot{\widehat x}&=k_g\widehat\omega(x-\widehat x)-\widehat\omega\widehat x',&
\dot{\widehat x}'&=\widehat\omega\widehat x,\notag\\
\frac{\widehat X(s)}{X(s)}&=\frac{k_g\widehat\omega s}{s^2+k_g\widehat\omega s+\widehat\omega^2},&
\frac{\widehat X'(s)}{X(s)}&=\frac{k_g\widehat\omega^2}{s^2+k_g\widehat\omega s+\widehat\omega^2}.
\label{eq:ch5-qsg}
\end{align}
Here $k_g>0$ controls estimator damping. At $s=j\widehat\omega$, the two transfer functions are $1$ and $-j$, respectively, giving the required lagging quadrature. A bilinear discretization with prewarping at the nominal grid frequency is one suitable realization. DC offsets require removal; the quadrature channel is not intrinsically DC rejecting. Frequency may be supplied by a stationary frequency estimator or set to the known nominal value when its variation is negligible. No electrical angle or Park transformation is required.

The IOPC matrix is formed from these fundamental estimates. The predictive plant model uses the sampled grid voltage, with the forecast described below. Thus the power-reference calculation targets fundamental power oscillations; it does not assert cancellation of all harmonic power. The explicit reconstructed-voltage estimator is selected as the baseline because it retains filter resistance in the plant and avoids making \eqref{eq:ch5-li-substitution} an assumed transient identity. This introduces additional estimator dynamics compared with Li et al.'s substitution. Its bandwidth, noise response, and phase error must be included in validation.

\section{Proposed Stationary-Frame IOPC--MMPCC Architecture}
\label{sec:ch5-architecture}
\subsection{Overall Operating Principle and DC-Link Regulation}
The outer controller regulates DC-link energy indirectly through the input active-power reference. The IOPC stage determines the current waveform needed to realize that reference while targeting zero mean input quadrature reactive power and zero fundamental output-power ripple. The MMPCC stage controls how the available switching states reproduce the required current. Figure~\ref{fig:ch5-architecture} separates these functions and identifies the historical converter-voltage path.

Let $\overline V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k]$ be a filtered DC-link measurement used by the outer loop and let $\varepsilon_v[k]=V_{\mathrm{dc}}^\ast-\overline V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k]$. A PI regulator whose output has units of power is
\begin{align}
P_u[k]&=K_{p,v}\varepsilon_v[k]+\xi[k],\notag\\
P_b[k]&=\operatorname{sat}_{[P_{\min},P_{\max}]}(P_u[k]),\notag\\
\xi[k+1]&=\xi[k]+T_s\{K_{i,v}\varepsilon_v[k]+K_{\mathrm{aw}}(P_{\mathrm{ref}}[k]-P_u[k])\}.
\label{eq:ch5-pi}
\end{align}
$P_b$ is the bounded request before the current-envelope limiter; $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the final accepted request. The gains have units $K_{p,v}$ in W/V, $K_{i,v}$ in W/(V\,s), and $K_{\mathrm{aw}}$ in $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This power-output PI is a deliberate implementation choice. It does not require treating the original paper's PI output as power if that output is instead a DC current command multiplied by the measured DC voltage.

At steady state, the integrator supplies the mean filter and bridge losses as well as load power. From Chapter~4, \eqref{eq:dc}, zero output-power ripple prevents the corresponding forced voltage ripple only when the load power and losses are compatible. The outer loop should regulate the average voltage rather than command a large double-frequency modulation of $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$. Its bandwidth and any measurement notch or low-pass filter must be designed jointly with the reference generator; no numerical tuning is asserted without the plant parameters.

\subsection{Causal Converter-Voltage Feedback and Sampling Timing}
Let $t_k=kT_s$ and let $\mathbf u[k]$ denote the average converter voltage scheduled for $[t_k,t_{k+1})$. With a one-period computation delay, $\mathbf u[k]$ is already committed when measurements are acquired at $t_k$. The command calculated at $t_k$ is $\mathbf u[k+1]$, to be applied during the following interval. This convention is used consistently in every prediction below.

The average voltage actually applied in the most recently completed interval can be reconstructed from the stored duty fractions and DC-link voltage:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf u_{\mathrm{rec}}[k-1]=\overline V_{\mathrm{dc}}^{\mathrm{sw}}[k-1]
\left(d_1[k-1]\mathbf s_{m[k-1]}+d_2[k-1]\mathbf s_{j[k-1]}\right),
\label{eq:ch5-reconstruction}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf s_m$ is the dimensionless Clarke switching vector and $\overline V_{\mathrm{dc}}^{\mathrm{sw}}$ is the measured interval voltage estimate used for reconstruction, not necessarily the slow outer-loop filtered value. Dead time and device drops can be included in a calibrated reconstruction.

At $t_k$, the converter-voltage signal-generator state is advanced causally using the completed-interval data. Its outputs $\widehat{\mathbf v}[k],\widehat{\mathbf v}'[k]$ are estimates of the fundamental terminal waveform at the current sampling time. They are not measurements of an unknown future optimized voltage. The grid signal generator is timestamped consistently. Fundamental estimator lag, interval averaging, and reconstruction error must be assessed; calling the outputs current-time estimates does not make those errors vanish.

The reference generator then forms \eqref{eq:ch5-iopc-matrix} from these estimates and the accepted power request. Its output is frozen during the six-pair predictive optimization. The optimizer is therefore not feeding a same-sample unknown voltage back into the IOPC solve. The one-period delayed feedback makes the implementation causal but changes the transient system relative to an ideal algebraic steady-state solution. Ripple cancellation follows only to the extent that the estimators, tracked references, and eventual converter fundamental voltage become mutually consistent.

\subsection{Reference Limiting and Future-Reference Evaluation}
The full four-component solve supplies both $\mathbf i^\ast$ and $\mathbf i^{\prime\ast}$. To limit the fundamental phase-current envelope, define the inverse Clarke matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-1/2&\sqrt3/2\\-1/2&-\sqrt3/2\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf a=\mathbf F\mathbf i^\ast,\quad\mathbf a'=\mathbf F\mathbf i^{\prime\ast}.
\label{eq:ch5-inverse-clarke}
\end{equation}
Solve first with $P_b$, then use
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{env}}=\max_{\ell\in\{a,b,c\}}\sqrt{a_\ell^2+a_\ell'^2},\quad
\gamma=\min\!\left(1,\frac{I_{\mathrm{ref,max}}}{I_{\mathrm{env}}}\right),\quad
P_{\mathrm{ref}}=\gamma P_b,\quad\mathbf z_{\mathrm{lim}}^\ast=\gamma\mathbf z^\ast.
\label{eq:ch5-reference-limiter}
\end{equation}
Set $\gamma=1$ if $I_{\mathrm{env}}=0$. A common scalar preserves the waveform shape and, for the frozen voltage matrix, scales its active-power row while keeping the three homogeneous rows zero. Independent clipping of phase or axis references does not preserve those conditions. Variation of $\gamma$ during transients introduces extra spectral content; exact steady-state power cancellation is not claimed during limiting changes.

For a fixed fundamental waveform, the future reference is obtained without a rotating coordinate transform:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf r[k+h]=\mathbf i_{\mathrm{lim}}^\ast[k]\cos(h\widehat\omega T_s)
-\mathbf i_{\mathrm{lim}}^{\prime\ast}[k]\sin(h\widehat\omega T_s),\qquad h=1,2.
\label{eq:ch5-reference-forecast}
\end{equation}
The minus sign follows from the lagging quadrature definition. This operation advances each scalar waveform in time; it does not rotate the $\alpha\beta$ axes. In particular, it works for an unbalanced fundamental waveform without imposing a single positive-sequence space-vector rotation. During power-reference changes it is a frozen-envelope forecast, not an exact prediction. Rate limiting and estimator-validity checks are therefore required. The grid fundamental is forecast analogously for $\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1]$; a sample-and-hold forecast is an alternative when its prediction error is acceptable.

\section{Stationary-Frame Model and Modulated Predictive Current Control}
\label{sec:ch5-mmpcc}
\subsection{Continuous-Time Model and Forward-Euler Discretization}
With equal filter parameters and current directed into the rectifier, Chapter~4, \eqref{eq:model}, becomes
\begin{equation}
L\dot{\mathbf i}=\mathbf e-R\mathbf i-\mathbf v,
\qquad
\begin{cases}
L\dot i_\alpha=e_\alpha-Ri_\alpha-v_\alpha,\\
L\dot i_\beta=e_\beta-Ri_\beta-v_\beta.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ch5-plant}
\end{equation}
The grid voltage drives the current, while the resistive drop and converter counter-voltage reduce its rate of increase. There are no frame-rotation cross-coupling terms. This equation is the direct stationary plant model, not a relabeling of a synchronous-frame predictor.

Integrating over one short sampling interval and holding its right-hand side at the sampling instant yields
\begin{align}
\mathbf i[k+1]&\simeq a\mathbf i[k]+b(\mathbf e[k]-\mathbf u[k]),\notag\\
a&=1-\frac{RT_s}{L},\qquad b=\frac{T_s}{L},
\label{eq:ch5-fe}\\
i_\alpha[k+1]&\simeq\left(1-\frac{RT_s}{L}\right)i_\alpha[k]
+\frac{T_s}{L}(e_\alpha[k]-u_\alpha[k]),\notag\\
i_\beta[k+1]&\simeq\left(1-\frac{RT_s}{L}\right)i_\beta[k]
+\frac{T_s}{L}(e_\beta[k]-u_\beta[k]).
\label{eq:ch5-fe-components}
\end{align}
Here $a$ is dimensionless and $b$ has units A/V. Forward Euler has local truncation error of order $T_s^2$ for smooth averaged dynamics. For the isolated resistive current pole, numerical stability requires $0<RT_s/L<2$ when $R>0$; useful prediction accuracy normally requires a much smaller ratio and $\omega T_s\ll1$. This numerical condition is not a stability proof for the complete controller. The switching-average approximation also neglects the exact ordering of subinterval voltages within the PWM period.

\subsection{Voltage Vectors of the Two-Level AFE}
For $\mathbf S=[S_a\ S_b\ S_c]^T\in\{0,1\}^3$, removing common mode and applying \eqref{eq:ch5-clarke} gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf v(\mathbf S)=V_{\mathrm{dc}}\begin{bmatrix}(2S_a-S_b-S_c)/3\\(S_b-S_c)/\sqrt3\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:ch5-switching-map}
\end{equation}
The six active vectors have magnitude $2V_{\mathrm{dc}}/3$ and are separated by $\pi/3$. The states $000$ and $111$ both give zero stationary voltage, although their switching transitions and common-mode behavior differ. Table~\ref{tab:ch5-vectors} fixes the indexing used throughout.

\subsection{Delay-Compensated Current Prediction}
If a command could be applied immediately, its one-step candidate prediction would be
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,m}[k+1]=a\mathbf i[k]+b(\mathbf e[k]-\mathbf v_m[k]),
\label{eq:ch5-one-step}
\end{equation}
and it would be compared with $\mathbf r[k+1]$. Under the selected one-period scheduling delay, the first step instead uses the already committed voltage:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf i}_1=\widehat{\mathbf i}[k+1]
=a\mathbf i[k]+b(\mathbf e[k]-\mathbf u_{\mathrm{ap}}[k]).
\label{eq:ch5-delay-step}
\end{equation}
$\mathbf u_{\mathrm{ap}}[k]$ is reconstructed from the committed duty pattern using the latest DC-link voltage estimate. It is not the newly optimized command. The second step uses the candidate for $[t_{k+1},t_{k+2})$:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,m}[k+2]
=a\widehat{\mathbf i}_1+b(\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1]-\mathbf v_m[k+1])
=\mathbf c-b\mathbf v_m[k+1],
\label{eq:ch5-two-step}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\mathbf c=a\widehat{\mathbf i}_1+b\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1],\qquad
\mathbf v_m[k+1]=\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k+1]\mathbf s_m.
\label{eq:ch5-base-prediction}
\end{equation}
The baseline DC-voltage forecast is $\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k+1]=V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k]$. A better forecast may be introduced if its extra uncertainty is justified. Every candidate is evaluated against the same target $\mathbf r[k+2]$ from \eqref{eq:ch5-reference-forecast}. Holding a stationary current reference unchanged would produce a phase error even in a balanced steady state; matching the prediction and reference horizons avoids that indexing error.

\subsection{Modulated Prediction and Required Average Voltage}
For pair $m$, let $j=m+1$ for $m=1,\ldots,5$ and $j=1$ for $m=6$. Define $\mathbf B_m=[\mathbf v_m\ \mathbf v_j]$ at the forecast DC voltage. With $d_0=1-d_1-d_2$, the average converter voltage is
\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathbf v}_m=\mathbf B_m\mathbf d,\qquad
\mathbf d=[d_1\ d_2]^T,\qquad d_1\ge0,\ d_2\ge0,\ d_1+d_2\le1.
\label{eq:ch5-average-voltage}
\end{equation}
Using the zero-vector prediction $\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,0}=mathbf c$, the modulated endpoint prediction is
\begin{align}
\widehat{\mathbf i}_m[k+2;\mathbf d]
&=d_0\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,0}+d_1\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,m}+d_2\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,j}\notag\\
&=\mathbf c-b\mathbf B_m\mathbf d.
\label{eq:ch5-modulated-prediction}
\end{align}
This duty-weighted identity is exact for the affine Forward-Euler averaged predictor. It is not an exact switched-waveform solution with finite subinterval dynamics.

Setting this prediction equal to the target gives the unconstrained required voltage
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\mathbf v_{\mathrm{db}}[k+1]
=\frac{\mathbf c-\mathbf r[k+2]}{b}
=\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1]+\frac{L}{T_s}
\left(a\widehat{\mathbf i}_1-\mathbf r[k+2]\right).}
\label{eq:ch5-deadbeat-voltage}
\end{equation}
A converter voltage larger than this value decreases the predicted grid current because converter voltage enters \eqref{eq:ch5-plant} with a negative sign. In the no-delay case, replace $\widehat{\mathbf i}_1,\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1],\mathbf r[k+2]$ with $\mathbf i[k],\mathbf e[k],\mathbf r[k+1]$. The resulting deadbeat equality is a nominal one-step property, not a claim of exact closed-loop tracking with model mismatch.

\subsection{Explicit Duty Cycles and Feasibility}
For a nonsingular adjacent pair, $\mathbf B_m\mathbf d=\mathbf v_{\mathrm{db}}$ gives
\begin{align}
\Delta_m&=v_{\alpha m}v_{\beta j}-v_{\alpha j}v_{\beta m}
=\frac{2\sqrt3}{9}\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}^2>0,\label{eq:ch5-pair-determinant}\\
d_1^{u}&=\frac{v_{\mathrm{db},\alpha}v_{\beta j}-v_{\alpha j}v_{\mathrm{db},\beta}}{\Delta_m},\qquad
 d_2^{u}=\frac{v_{\alpha m}v_{\mathrm{db},\beta}-v_{\mathrm{db},\alpha}v_{\beta m}}{\Delta_m},\label{eq:ch5-unconstrained-duties}\\
d_0^{u}&=1-d_1^{u}-d_2^{u}.\label{eq:ch5-zero-duty}
\end{align}
The determinant sign follows the counterclockwise indexing, including pair $(6,1)$. The numerators and denominator both have units of voltage squared, so the duties are dimensionless. No absolute values are taken: a negative duty identifies a voltage outside that pair's triangle and is essential feasibility information.

The union of the six triangles is the available voltage hexagon. Its inscribed circle has radius $\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}/\sqrt3$, a sufficient bound for a rotating reference at every angle. The hexagon is the exact average-voltage feasible set under the ideal two-level model. The active-vector radius $2\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}/3$ is not an all-angle linear-modulation limit. References outside a triangle, or outside the hexagon, require constrained optimization rather than taking absolute duties or independently clipping and renormalizing them.

\subsection{Current-Tracking Objective and Hard Constraints}
For each pair, minimize the predicted endpoint error:
\begin{equation}
J_m(\mathbf d)=
\left(\mathbf r[k+2]-\widehat{\mathbf i}_m[k+2;\mathbf d]\right)^T
\mathbf W
\left(\mathbf r[k+2]-\widehat{\mathbf i}_m[k+2;\mathbf d]\right),
\quad \mathbf W=\frac{1}{I_b^2}\mathbf I_2.
\label{eq:ch5-cost}
\end{equation}
$I_b>0$ is a current normalization base. Equal axis weights avoid an arbitrary orientation preference; a specified positive diagonal matrix is possible if unequal errors have a physical justification. No switching penalty is required to obtain the prescribed PWM update period.

A weighted sum of separate full-period vector costs generally differs from the cost of their modulated current. For squared errors, convexity gives $J(\sum d_\ell\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,\ell})\le\sum d_\ell J(\widehat{\mathbf i}^{\,\ell})$. The two expressions can rank pairs differently. Equation~\eqref{eq:ch5-cost} therefore evaluates the actual averaged predictor, rather than importing a heuristic sum of local errors from another formulation.

The sampled phase-current constraint is
\begin{equation}
-I_{\mathrm{lim}}\mathbf1\ \le\ \mathbf F(\mathbf c-b\mathbf B_m\mathbf d)
\ \le\ I_{\mathrm{lim}}\mathbf1.
\label{eq:ch5-phase-constraint}
\end{equation}
Together with the three duty inequalities, it defines a convex polygon $\mathcal P_m$ in the $(d_1,d_2)$ plane. $I_{\mathrm{lim}}$ is chosen below the hardware trip limit to allow for model uncertainty and PWM ripple. These are hard constraints on the predicted sampled endpoint, not proof that the current remains bounded at every instant inside the switching period. The already committed interval must also be checked through $\mathbf F\widehat{\mathbf i}_1$; a new command cannot correct a violation that occurs before it is applied.

\subsection{Exact Two-Variable Constrained Optimization}
Because $\mathbf B_m$ is nonsingular at a valid DC voltage, the unconstrained solution is $\mathbf d^u=\mathbf B_m^{-1}\mathbf v_{\mathrm{db}}$. Substituting \eqref{eq:ch5-deadbeat-voltage} into the cost gives
\begin{equation}
J_m(\mathbf d)=(\mathbf d-\mathbf d^u)^T\mathbf H_m(\mathbf d-\mathbf d^u),\qquad
\mathbf H_m=b^2\mathbf B_m^T\mathbf W\mathbf B_m\succ0.
\label{eq:ch5-hessian}
\end{equation}
Start with the duty triangle and clip it by the six half-planes in \eqref{eq:ch5-phase-constraint}. If the resulting polygon is empty, reject the pair. If $\mathbf d^u\in\mathcal P_m$, it is the solution. Otherwise, the unique minimizer lies on the polygon boundary. For every edge from vertex $\mathbf p$ to $\mathbf q$, set $\mathbf g=\mathbf q-\mathbf p$ and calculate
\begin{equation}
\eta^\ast=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}
\frac{\mathbf g^T\mathbf H_m(\mathbf d^u-\mathbf p)}{\mathbf g^T\mathbf H_m\mathbf g},\qquad
\mathbf d_{\mathrm{edge}}=\mathbf p+\eta^\ast\mathbf g.
\label{eq:ch5-edge-projection}
\end{equation}
Evaluate the cost at these edge candidates and vertices, retaining the minimum. Repeated vertices and zero-length edges are removed. A polygon reduced to one vertex or one segment is handled directly. This construction solves the convex pair problem globally under the stated averaged model and constraints; it also provides a deterministic treatment of an infeasible voltage demand. A small two-variable quadratic-program solver is an equivalent implementation.

The current limit and the duty constraints have distinct roles. The former bounds predicted current, while the latter enforces achievable average voltage. A current-reference limiter does not replace either constraint. If every pair is infeasible, no admissible command exists for that endpoint within the model. The controller must signal its protection supervisor and use a prequalified hardware-specific response; choosing a zero vector is not automatically safe because the grid may continue to drive current through the filter and diodes.

\subsection{Global Pair Selection and Symmetric SVM}
After solving all nonempty pair problems, select
\begin{equation}
m^\ast\in\underset{m:\mathcal P_m\ne\varnothing}{\arg\min}\ J_m(\mathbf d_m^\ast),\qquad
\mathbf u[k+1]=\mathbf B_{m^\ast}\mathbf d_{m^\ast}^\ast,\quad
 d_0^\ast=1-d_1^\ast-d_2^\ast.
\label{eq:ch5-global-selection}
\end{equation}
Since the six triangles cover the hexagon, this is a global optimum of the single-endpoint current-tracking problem over that hexagon and the sampled-current constraints. It is not an optimum over arbitrary multi-step switching sequences. At a sector boundary, two pairs can represent the same voltage and cost; a deterministic tie rule may retain the preceding sector without changing the minimum.

The duties are converted to a symmetric seven-segment switching sequence. To ensure one-leg transitions, call the active state with one high switch $\mathbf S_A$ and that with two high switches $\mathbf S_B$, regardless of the pair's counterclockwise order. Associate their respective duties $d_A,d_B$ with the optimized vectors. The sequence and segment durations are
\begin{equation}
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
\text{state}&000&\mathbf S_A&\mathbf S_B&111&\mathbf S_B&\mathbf S_A&000\\
\text{duration}/T_s&d_0/4&d_A/2&d_B/2&d_0/2&d_B/2&d_A/2&d_0/4
\end{array}
\label{eq:ch5-svm-sequence}
\end{equation}
Their sum is one and their average voltage is \eqref{eq:ch5-average-voltage}. The PWM frame repeats at $1/T_s$ under the assumed single-rate implementation. Individual devices can have missing transitions at zero duty, saturation, or sector boundaries; fixed PWM timing does not mean an identical number of transitions for every device at every operating point. Minimum pulse widths, dead time, and timer quantization perturb the requested duties and must be reflected in the stored applied command and reconstruction.

\section{Complete Proposed IOPC--MMPCC Algorithm}
\label{sec:ch5-algorithm}
The following sequence implements the one-period delayed controller. The signal-generator states, stored PWM commands, and PI integrator are persistent variables. All electrical control calculations remain in stationary coordinates.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Initialize $T_s,R,L$, DC-voltage and power bounds, $I_b$, $I_{\mathrm{ref,max}}$, $I_{\mathrm{lim}}$, PI gains, signal-generator parameters, conditioning thresholds, and the switching-state table. Establish valid sensor offsets and a prequalified startup/protection sequence.
\item At $t_k$, acquire the three grid voltages, three grid currents, and DC-link voltage using synchronized sampling. Check sensor validity, DC-voltage range, and hardware fault status.
\item Apply \eqref{eq:ch5-clarke} to obtain $\mathbf e[k]$ and $\mathbf i[k]$. Check the three-wire current residual against the measurement tolerance.
\item Reconstruct the completed interval's converter voltage from the applied duties using \eqref{eq:ch5-reconstruction}. Advance the stationary signal generators and the frequency estimate, if one is used. Do not use a yet-unknown optimized voltage.
\item Calculate $P_u$ and $P_b$ from the filtered DC-link voltage using \eqref{eq:ch5-pi}. Defer the integrator update until the accepted power reference is known.
\item Assemble the IOPC matrix from timestamp-consistent fundamental voltage estimates. Check the row-scaled condition estimate and estimator validity. If invalid, suppress the new power request and invoke the specified supervisory response rather than dividing by a small determinant.
\item Solve \eqref{eq:ch5-iopc-matrix} with $P_b$ for both current components and their quadratures. Apply the common envelope limiter \eqref{eq:ch5-reference-limiter}; obtain $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and complete the anti-windup update in \eqref{eq:ch5-pi}.
\item Forecast $\mathbf r[k+2]$ using \eqref{eq:ch5-reference-forecast}. Forecast $\widehat{\mathbf e}[k+1]$ and $\widehat V_{\mathrm{dc}}[k+1]$ according to the selected signal models.
\item Reconstruct the already committed voltage for the present interval and compute $\widehat{\mathbf i}_1$ with \eqref{eq:ch5-delay-step}. Check whether the committed interval already requires a protection response. Compute $\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf v_{\mathrm{db}}$.
\item Initialize the best cost to infinity. For $m=1,\ldots,6$, form pair $(m,j)$ with $j=m+1$ and wrap $j=1$ when $m=6$.
\item Calculate the two active-vector predictions and the zero-vector prediction using \eqref{eq:ch5-two-step}. Form $\mathbf B_m$, its determinant, and the unconstrained duties in \eqref{eq:ch5-unconstrained-duties}.
\item Construct $\mathcal P_m$ from the duty triangle and sampled phase-current inequalities. Reject an empty polygon. Otherwise solve its constrained duty problem using \eqref{eq:ch5-hessian} and \eqref{eq:ch5-edge-projection}.
\item Verify the nonnegative duties, their unit sum including $d_0$, and the predicted phase-current bounds within numerical tolerance. Evaluate \eqref{eq:ch5-cost} at the resulting modulated current and update the best pair if its cost is smaller.
\item After all six pairs, select the minimum-cost feasible pair using \eqref{eq:ch5-global-selection}. Resolve numerical ties deterministically. If none is feasible, signal the protection supervisor; do not apply an unverified fallback vector.
\item Convert the chosen duties into the symmetric SVM sequence \eqref{eq:ch5-svm-sequence}, accounting for timer resolution and permitted minimum pulse widths. Schedule the resulting sequence for $[t_{k+1},t_{k+2})$.
\item Store the scheduled states, realized duties, DC-voltage estimate, signal-generator states, limiter state, and accepted power request. Monitor persistent voltage saturation to curtail the outer-loop demand when necessary. Repeat at $t_{k+1}$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Practical Design Considerations and Limitations}
\label{sec:ch5-limitations}
\subsection{Current and Voltage Constraints}
The converter voltage is limited by the measured DC voltage and the switching hexagon, not by an unlimited continuous voltage command. The fundamental current envelope limiter reduces $P_{\mathrm{ref}}$ when a requested current exceeds the allowed phase amplitude. The pair optimization then imposes endpoint current limits and voltage feasibility simultaneously. Persistent nonzero tracking error caused by voltage saturation should trigger a reduction in the allowable power request or DC-side load demand. The required supervisory law depends on the actual load and protection scheme and cannot be selected from the supplied chapter fragments alone.

Neither the envelope limiter nor a sampled prediction is a substitute for hardware overcurrent protection. Intersample ripple, saturation of the filter inductors, dead time, and measurement uncertainty require margin. If current is already outside the safe operating region, a complete protection design must account for the uncontrolled diode conduction paths and grid energy. The normal control algorithm is not a fault-isolation design.

\subsection{Matrix Conditioning and Severe Voltage Dips}
The sum-of-squares form in \eqref{eq:ch5-det-factorization} identifies exact singularity but has units of voltage to the fourth power. A fixed dimensional determinant threshold is therefore not portable across voltage ratings. Scale each nonzero row of $\mathbf M$ by a voltage magnitude and evaluate a dimensionless condition estimate; reject rows below a minimum voltage threshold before normalizing. Use a threshold based on sensor accuracy and the numerical precision of the target processor.

Near singularity, small voltage-estimation errors can produce large current-reference errors. Scaling the active-power request can reduce current magnitude, but it does not restore rank or eliminate poor conditioning. A pseudoinverse or regularized solve would change the exact IOPC objectives and would need to be identified as a separate degraded operating mode. The baseline design instead detects the condition and transfers control to the supervisor. Recovery requires valid estimator states and a gradual reintroduction of power demand.

\subsection{DC-Link Regulation and Anti-Windup}
The power command must be compatible with both the AC converter limits and the DC-side energy balance. The anti-windup feedback in \eqref{eq:ch5-pi} uses the accepted, current-limited power request rather than only the first saturation output. Persistent voltage saturation is an additional reason to curtail demand; it should not be hidden by indefinite PI integration. During a deep dip, the available AC power may be lower than the load power, so maintaining the DC voltage may be physically impossible even with perfect current control.

The outer-loop filter should attenuate the double-frequency component enough to avoid reintroducing it into the reference envelope. Excessive filtering slows response to load changes. The appropriate bandwidth separation depends on the DC-link capacitance, operating voltage, load model, signal-generator dynamics, and inner-loop sampling. These parameters must be supplied before selecting gains or claiming a stability margin.

\subsection{Predictive-Model Accuracy}
Errors in $L$ change the predicted effect of an applied voltage; errors in $R$ bias the resistive-drop prediction. The two-step horizon accumulates both measurement and modeling errors. Grid-voltage forecast errors become particularly important at dip inception and during waveform distortion. Changes in $V_{\mathrm{dc}}$ modify every active-vector amplitude, while dead time and device drops cause the realized converter voltage to differ from the ideal vector average.

The committed present-interval command must be distinguished from the historical completed-interval command used to update the voltage estimator. Confusing these indices can introduce an additional delay. Synchronized measurements, calibrated reconstruction, and consistent timestamps are therefore part of the mathematical implementation, not merely software details. More accurate discretization or parameter adaptation may be added, but their effects must be assessed within the same delayed closed loop.

\subsection{Digital Implementation and Computational Budget}
The principal operations per sample are four scalar quadrature generators, a small four-variable reference solve, one delay-compensation prediction, and six two-variable constrained optimizations. Polygon clipping uses a fixed number of half-planes, so the maximum problem size is bounded. Matrix conditioning and protective checks must be included when measuring the worst-case execution time. Completing an average iteration within $T_s$ is insufficient if a difficult saturation case exceeds the available PWM update deadline.

Li et al. discuss sequence-extraction and frequency-processing approaches in \cite{ch5-santos2009,ch5-yazdani2009,ch5-timbus2006}, and feedforward control under more general unbalance in \cite{ch5-stankovic2009}. These references identify relevant extensions, rather than providing a validation of the present implementation. Their related digital-control publications~\cite{ch5-li2008gpu,ch5-li2010gpu} concern a different power-converter application and do not establish execution time or tuning for this AFE controller.

The nominal switching pattern should be translated into timer counts, checked for minimum pulse widths, and stored as actually scheduled. If pulse suppression changes the duty fractions, the applied voltage should be reconstructed from those modified fractions. A controller that stores the requested rather than realized command will systematically mispredict its next interval.

\subsection{Control-Objective Trade-Offs and Scope of the Proposal}
A three-wire fundamental current waveform contains four real coefficients. IOPC uses them to impose one average active-power target, one average quadrature reactive-power target, and two output ripple conditions. Balanced currents, zero input active-power ripple, zero output active-power ripple, zero instantaneous reactive power, and constant DC voltage cannot be independently imposed under arbitrary unbalance using these same degrees of freedom.

The current-reference equations are exact algebraic statements for the specified fundamental voltage waveforms. Their causal implementation uses estimated historical converter voltage, while the predictive regulator operates with a finite sampling period and a constrained average-voltage model. Consequently, this chapter establishes an implementable nominal control law and the conditions under which its intended steady-state power objectives are consistent. It does not establish global closed-loop stability, convergence of the estimator--IOPC feedback, fault ride-through compliance, or a numerical harmonic-performance improvement.

Subsequent validation should examine feasible steady-state equilibria, estimator convergence, frequency deviation, voltage dips, load changes, parameter mismatch, pulse constraints, and worst-case execution time. Validation should also measure both reactive-power definitions and a clearly specified true power-factor metric. These checks are required to distinguish successful current tracking from successful realization of the selected power objectives.
